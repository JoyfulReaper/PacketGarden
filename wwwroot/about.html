About PacketGarden

Packet Garden: Composting the internet's shit into something pretty.

Back to the garden

What is this?

PacketGarden is a living visualization of background network activity. Instead of turning every connection and traffic spike into another table, graph, or scrolling log, it turns them into flowers, mushrooms, vines, weeds, strange mutations, weather, and occasionally a completely unnecessary gnome territorial dispute.

It is meant to be something you can leave open and glance at. The garden slowly changes as traffic arrives, plants age and die, grass spreads, storms roll through, and odd little events happen on their own.

How does it work?

Network sensors → NATS → PacketGarden backend → SSE → Your browser garden

TCPNoise sensors observe connections to listening ports and publish small event summaries. PacketGarden consumes those events through NATS, normalizes them on the server, and streams only the information the browser needs over Server-Sent Events (SSE).

The browser then grows the appropriate specimen, chooses its visual details, updates the ecology, and optionally plays a short sound. No page refresh or direct browser connection to the sensors is required.

What do the plants mean?

Ports are mapped to plant families for visual variety. Common web-facing traffic tends to produce broad-leaf plants, Gopher/port 70 can produce mushrooms, several remote-access style ports map to vines, and other ports commonly become flowers. Port 22 has its own brief red bloom so those hits are easy to notice.

These are artistic mappings, not protocol detection and not security classifications. A red bloom means a TCP connection hit the public listener on port 22; it does not mean somebody supplied credentials, completed SSH negotiation, or successfully attacked anything.

Vigor, rarity, and weird colors

Repeated observations are reduced to a coarse vigor value from 0 to 3. Higher vigor makes specimens a little fuller or more detailed and, when every specimen cell is occupied, can allow stronger recurring growth to displace weaker ordinary plants.

TCPNoise also has an artistic rarity roll that can produce weeds or rare species such as ghostblooms, crystal fungi, and blackthorns. Separately, specimens have browser-side color variation. Common colors follow vigor for live traffic, while very occasionally a specimen gets a rare cosmetic color scheme. A rainbow broad-leaf plant is one example. Color rarity is just decoration and does not change the network meaning of the event.

Grass and ecology

Grass is its own layer beneath the specimens. Development DNS and HTTP events can seed or refresh it, and healthy mature grass can spread naturally. Old turf fades, browns, and eventually disappears, with a small final chance to seed a neighbor before it goes.

Plants also have their own lifetimes. The result is intentionally temporary: the garden keeps recycling itself rather than becoming a permanent historical database of everything that ever touched the network.

What is the weather?

Weather comes from aggregate activity rather than individual connection events. The backend polls Noise Observatory for a current level and events-per-minute rate, then streams that small weather state to the browser. Higher activity can turn drizzle into heavy rain, thunderstorms, or a botnet hurricane.

Weather is currently visual. It does not damage plants or change the meaning of traffic.

And the gnomes?

The gnomes mean absolutely nothing.

Once the garden is sufficiently populated, there is a small chance of a browser-only ambient event in which two tiny red-hat gnomes and two green-hat gnomes settle a pointless territorial disagreement. They do not consume plant cells, alter traffic, classify threats, or affect the outcome of anything important. They just fight and leave.

Privacy

The browser does not receive raw packet contents, credentials, sensor names, remote IP addresses, or exact repeat counts. Live specimen events are reduced to the visual specimen type, listening port, and coarse vigor value needed to render the garden.

PacketGarden is an ambient visualization, not a packet inspector, SIEM, IDS, or forensic tool. If something looks ominous, that is because the garden is dramatic, not because it has decided somebody is malicious.

Why?

Because the public internet produces an endless amount of unsolicited background noise, and watching it grow mushrooms is more fun than staring at another dashboard.
